# package an offer by value driver

Use when a technical capability is interesting but the buyer value, price anchor, or first paid step is unclear.

## 1. Name one buyer and one job

Write the buyer role and the painful job in plain language. If the same offer claims to serve everyone, split it.

Output:

```md
Buyer:
Painful job:
Current workaround:
Why now:
```

## 2. Classify the value driver

Choose the main reason the buyer would pay:

- speed,
- friction removal,
- risk reduction,
- revenue or capacity,
- learning or confidence.

Do not lead with the mechanism. Mechanisms can change; the value driver is what the buyer can defend.

## 3. Anchor against the real alternative

Capture the current workaround, cost of delay, cost of inaction, and expensive alternative. A price with no alternative feels arbitrary; a package compared with the current pain can be evaluated.

## 4. Shrink the first paid step

When trust is low, sell a diagnostic, audit, prototype, migration plan, workbench setup, or first proof artifact. The first step should create evidence and de-risk the larger engagement.

## 5. Attach proof and failure points

List the proof artifact and the likely failure points before proposing the full package. Trust rises when the offer states what can go wrong and what the operator will do if it does.

## 6. Write the offer scope card

Use [the offer scope card template](../templates/offer-scope-card.md). Refuse to price from effort alone until the card names the buyer, job, value driver, anchor, proof, risks, and first paid step.

## 7. Verification

The package is ready when a reviewer can answer:

- Who buys this?
- What painful job changes?
- Is the value speed, friction, risk, revenue, or learning?
- What is the current alternative?
- What proof exists?
- What first paid step is small enough to earn trust?
- What happens if delivery fails?

Related law: [SHIP-12](../notes/06-shipping.md#ship-12-buyers-pay-for-a-resolved-constraint-not-a-mechanism).
